Our analysis of 33,596 AI-generated pull requests shows that AI agents typically make small, focused code contributions that follow good software engineering practices. The median pull request (PR) adds only 72 lines of code, removes 10, and changes just 2 files. Most PRs are compact: 57.1% are Small or Extra-Small (fewer than 100 lines added), and 38.9% fall in the ideal range of 10–100 added lines. This pattern suggests that AI agents tend to make incremental, targeted updates instead of large, sweeping changes—an approach that supports easier code review and lowers the chance of bugs or merge conflicts. The fact that the typical PR affects only a couple of files (median of 2, with 75% touching 7 or fewer) also shows that AI contributions are highly localized, making them easier for reviewers to understand.

Even though most PRs are small, the dataset shows strong variation due to a few very large ones. The average number of added lines (2,064) and deleted lines (1,179) are much higher than the medians because of extreme outliers—one PR even added over 2.5 million lines of code. The large standard deviations (28,568 for additions and 20,565 for deletions) and high 95th-percentile values (3,743 additions, 1,482 deletions, 58 files) highlight this uneven distribution. These big PRs likely represent special cases such as dependency updates, generated code, large refactors, or migrations—very different from the smaller, focused feature changes that make up most of the data. This mix of small and large PRs suggests that different review approaches may be needed depending on PR size.

Looking at the ratio of code added to code removed gives more insight into what AI agents are doing. Across all PRs, there are 69.3 million lines added and 39.6 million deleted, an overall ratio of about 1.75:1. On a per-PR basis, the median ratio is even higher at roughly 7:1, showing that most agentic PRs are heavily biased toward adding new code rather than modifying or removing existing code. This pattern suggests AI agents are mainly being used for building new features and expanding codebases rather than refactoring or cleaning up old code. The strong focus on additive changes may reflect both what current AI tools are best at and the kinds of tasks developers choose to assign to them. Future research could explore how this bias toward adding code affects code quality, maintainability, and review effectiveness.
